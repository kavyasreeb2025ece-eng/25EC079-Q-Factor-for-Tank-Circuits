1. Introduction

A tank circuit is an inductor (L) and capacitor (C) connected so that energy oscillates between the magnetic field of L and the electric field of C at the resonant frequency. Real losses (coil resistance, load, source resistance) damp the oscillation. The quality factor Q measures how lightly damped the tank is.

Q = 2π × (maximum energy stored) / (energy dissipated per cycle) = f0 / BW

Why it matters

Q sets selectivity (how sharply a radio picks one station), bandwidth, ringing/settling time, oscillator phase noise and the gain of tuned amplifiers and active band-pass filters.

Prerequisites

Complex impedance/admittance, phasors, series/parallel R-L-C behaviour, decibels, basic op-amp filters.

Terminology

TermMeaning
ResonanceFrequency where reactances cancel (XL = XC); the response is purely resistive
f0, ω0Resonant frequency (Hz) and angular frequency (rad/s)
Bandwidth (BW)f2 − f1 between half-power (−3 dB, 0.707 of peak) points
SelectivityAbility to separate nearby frequencies; ∝ Q
Unloaded / loaded QQ of the tank alone (Q0) / Q including source and load resistance (QL)

Types

Series resonant (impedance minimum at f0, current maximum) and parallel resonant (impedance maximum, voltage maximum). Further: low-Q (Q < 10), medium and high-Q (Q > 100, crystals, cavities).

3. Working Principle

  1. Below f0: XL < XC; L carries most current, tank is inductive, output small and leads input.
  2. At f0: inductor and capacitor currents are equal and opposite; the LC branch draws no net current from the source. Only R1 remains, so Vout is maximum and in phase with the source current. Circulating current inside the tank is Q times the line current.
  3. Above f0: C dominates; tank is capacitive, output falls and lags.

Energy view: energy swaps between L and C; resistance removes a fraction 2π/Q of the stored energy each cycle. Free oscillation decays as e−t/τ, τ = 2Q/ω0 (= 0.2 ms for the circuit above).

Assumptions: ideal L and C, resistance modelled as one equivalent resistor; Q ≫ 1 for the approximations f1,2 ≈ f0 ± BW/2 and Rp ≈ Q²r.

Advantages: simple, high selectivity, passive. Limitations: inductors are bulky and lossy, Q drops with source/load resistance, tolerance and temperature shift f0. Design: keep source impedance high or tap the tank (impedance transformation) to preserve Q.

Common misconceptions: (1) “Higher R always means higher Q” – true for parallel, false for series. (2) “Q is gain” – Q is a ratio of reactance to resistance; voltage magnification applies to series L/C voltages. (3) Ignoring source/load resistance (loaded Q ≠ coil Q). (4) Ideal parallel LC has zero impedance at resonance – wrong, it is infinite.

5. Equations and Derivations

Resonant frequency

ω0 = 1/√(LC)  ,  f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

From XL = XC → ω0L = 1/(ω0C). L in H, C in F, f in Hz.

Series RLC

Qs = ω0L/R = 1/(ω0RC) = (1/R)√(L/C)  ; BW = R/(2πL)

Derivation: at resonance I = V/R; VL = I·ω0L = Q·V. Half-power when |Z| = √2 R, i.e. |X| = R, giving ω2 − ω1 = R/L, so BW = ω0/Q.

Parallel RLC

Qp = R/(ω0L) = ω0RC = R√(C/L)  ; BW = 1/(2πRC)

Derivation: Y = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/ωL). Resonance: imaginary part 0, Y = G. Half-power when |B| = G, giving ω2 − ω1 = G/C = 1/(RC) = ω0/Q.

General results

BW = f0/Q  ; f1,2 ≈ f0 ∓ BW/2 (Q ≫ 1)  ; f0 = √(f1f2)
Real coil: QL = ωL/r  ; Rp = r(1+QL2) ≈ QL2r
Loading: 1/Rp,total = 1/Rp + 1/Rsource + 1/Rload → Qloaded = Rp,total/(ω0L)
1/Qloaded = 1/Qcoil + 1/Qexternal  ; τ = 2Q/ω0
dB = 20 log10|H| ; −3 dB ↔ 0.707 ; phase of Z at f1,2 = ±45°

Limiting cases: R→∞ (parallel) gives Q→∞ (lossless tank); R→0 (series) gives Q→∞. Physical meaning: Q is the ratio of reactance to resistance at ω0.

6. Solved Numerical Problems

Problem 1 (Easy) – Series RLC

Given R = 10 Ω, L = 1 mH, C = 1 µF. Find f0, Q, BW.

f0 = 1/(2π√(10−3·10−6)) = 1/(2π × 3.162×10−5) = 5.03 kHz.
Q = (1/10)√(10−3/10−6) = √1000/10 = 3.16.
BW = f0/Q = 5033/3.162 = 1.59 kHz (check: R/2πL = 10/6.283×10−3 = 1.59 kHz ✓).
Interpretation: low Q (≈3), a broad, weakly selective response.

Problem 2 (Moderate) – The Multisim tank

R1 = 10 kΩ, L = 10 mH, C = 10 nF, source resistance R2 = 100 kΩ. Find Q (unloaded and loaded), BW and gain at f0.

ω0 = 105 rad/s, f0 = 15.915 kHz; ω0L = 1 kΩ. Q0 = 10 kΩ/1 kΩ = 10. R1∥R2 = 9.09 kΩ → QL = 9.09; BW = 15.915 k/9.09 = 1.75 kHz (unloaded: 1.59 kHz). Gain at f0 = 10/110 = 0.0909.
Interpretation: the 100 kΩ source reduces Q by about 9% and widens BW; the signal is attenuated 20.8 dB at the peak.

Problem 3 (Difficult, GATE-style) – Coil Q and equivalent parallel resistance

A coil L = 100 µH has Q = 100 at 1 MHz. It is tuned to 1 MHz with a lossless capacitor. Find the coil resistance, C, and the tank impedance at resonance.

ωL = 2π×106×10−4 = 628.3 Ω → r = 628.3/100 = 6.28 Ω. C = 1/(ω²L) = 1/((6.283×106)²×10−4) = 253 pF. Rp ≈ Q²r = 104×6.28 = 62.8 kΩ.
Shortcut: Ztank,res = Q·ω0L = 100 × 628 Ω = 62.8 kΩ. Interpretation: a 6 Ω series loss looks like a 62.8 kΩ parallel resistor, so the source must be high-impedance to keep Q.

Quick revision

  • BW = f0/Q; half-power = 0.707 = −3 dB; phase ±45° there.
  • Memory aid: Series – Small R is high Q; Parallel – Plenty of R is high Q.
  • Loaded Q < unloaded Q; add conductances in parallel, resistances in series.
  • Real coil: Rp ≈ Q²r; Zres = Q·ω0L.
  • Decay time constant τ = 2Q/ω0; high Q = longer ringing.

Common errors & troubleshooting

  • Unit slips (nF vs µF, mH vs H) shift f0 by 10x – check f0 sanity first.
  • Simulating at a frequency far from f0 (as in the 1 kHz case above) and concluding the circuit is “wrong”.
  • Forgetting source/load resistance when computing Q.
  • Using Q ≫ 1 approximations for Q < 5 (f1,2 not symmetric).
  • Scope probe capacitance (≈10–15 pF) detunes small-C tanks and lowers measured Q.
